chapter 00

Neither hype nor doom

Most of what we hear about AI comes from two loud corners. One of them says it will change everything by next spring, and the other says it’s a bubble, a threat, or both at once. Most of us live somewhere in the middle, a little excited and a little uneasy, with a backlog that still needs clearing by Friday.

The two comfortable corners

What those corners have in common is that neither of them asks very much of you. Putting AI into every product and every workflow needs no real decision, because the answer is always yes. Refusing to touch it is just as comfortable, because the answer is always no. Both let you skip the slower work of looking at one specific problem, with real people and real constraints around it, and working out whether this tool makes it any better.

That slower work is what this guide is about. I’ve been calling it judgement in the loop.

We have been here before

At the end of the nineteenth century, factories ran on steam. A single large engine sat somewhere in the building and turned a long shaft, and every machine on the floor was connected to that shaft with belts. The whole factory was arranged around being close to the engine, whether or not that made sense for the work.

When electric motors arrived, most owners did the sensible-looking thing and replaced the steam engine with one big electric motor. The shaft, the belts and the layout all stayed exactly where they were, and productivity hardly moved for years. The real gains came much later, once people realised they could give each machine its own small motor. Then the floor no longer had to be built around a shaft, so factories could be laid out around the flow of the work instead, often on a single level with far more light. That’s when output finally rose.

The economist Paul David told this story in 1990 to explain why computers weren’t yet showing up in productivity figures, and it fits our moment almost too well. The technology was ready long before people knew what to do with it. The time went into working out how the work itself should change, which machines belonged where, and which jobs still needed someone standing beside them. Those were judgement calls, and they were made by people who understood both the old way of working and the new motors.

The motors arrived within a few years. Working out how to rearrange the work around them took decades.

What I mean by judgement in the loop

People who build AI systems often talk about keeping a human in the loop. Usually that means someone checks the output before it goes anywhere important. It’s a good habit, though over time it can turn a person into a rubber stamp, approving things at the end of a process they had no say in.

Judgement in the loop asks a little more of us. It means deciding what goes into the loop in the first place, what the loop is allowed to do on its own, and when something should come out of it altogether. That kind of judgement is hard to automate, because it depends on knowing your users, your team and what you’re actually trying to achieve, and those things rarely fit inside a prompt.

A short moment from your own work where judgement mattered more than the tool. The rollout where the login targets were hit and the work still got worse would fit well here. Two or three sentences, in your words.

How to read the rest

I build with AI most days and I enjoy it a lot, so this guide comes from wanting to use it well. Sometimes that means using it more than you expected, and sometimes it means leaving a task alone because the friction in it is where the thinking happens.

The chapters follow a rough path. They start with understanding what you’re working with, move on to shaping what it knows and what it can do, and end with choosing how much of the work to hand over. You can read them in order, or jump straight to whichever question is sitting on your desk this week. Each one closes with the same question, which is what should stay human, and the last chapter gives that question the space it deserves.

what stays human here

The decision about where AI belongs in your product, in your team and in your own working day. Tools can help you think it through, but the call, and the responsibility that comes with it, stays with you.
