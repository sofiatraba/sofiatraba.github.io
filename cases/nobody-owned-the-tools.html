Nobody owned the tools

Project

Role
Product Manager
When
Nov 2025–ongoing
Scale
Several thousand employees
Status
Migration shipped · operating model in build
Client
White-labelled

Several thousand people had to leave Slack for Teams. The licences were bought and the date was set before anyone asked how those people actually worked, and I was one of the people who had to go and tell them.

The software migration was the easy part.

Every message history, every integration, every workflow somebody had quietly built their day around: that was the work.

What was actually hard

The objections were not really about Slack. People had built working days on top of it, and most of that was invisible until we tried to move it. Alerts routed into a channel so nobody had to check three systems by hand. A pinned message doing the job of documentation nobody had written. Channels that were the only reason two departments spoke to each other.

The people who pushed back hardest were usually the ones whose workflow was load bearing and undocumented, which made them right, not difficult. Some of what they raised was a knowledge gap, where the new tool did the thing already and nobody knew. Some was a genuine product gap, where Teams simply does not work the way Slack did and no amount of enablement changes that. Telling those two apart was most of the job, because they look identical when they arrive.

And the organisation was tired. In under a year it had also decommissioned a project tool, migrated its service management, and restructured its knowledge base. The reaction to us was not disagreement so much as exhaustion.

What I did about it

We ran a grace period instead of a cutover. The old tool went read only in mid April and stayed reachable until mid May, so nobody had to trust us about what they would lose. They could go and look.

We were explicit that most of it was not coming with us. There was no automatic export. The guidance was that you move what your team genuinely needs going forward, and nothing else. That sounds harsh and it was the kindest option available, because the alternative is telling several thousand people to migrate everything and watching them discover in May that they cannot.

We pre-created the most used channels for every team before rollout, so nobody arrived at an empty room, and then made the rest self service so people could find and join what they needed without waiting on an owner. And we wrote documentation you could scan rather than read, from the user's point of view, updated the same day a real question surfaced rather than at the end of a sprint.

The decision I would not defend is that we briefed the team owners too late. Some of them got their setup guide after their own people had already started asking questions, which put the least prepared person in each team in front of the hardest conversations. Owner readiness should have been a gate on access.

The migration landed on time. Rollout started at the end of February, Slack went read only on 17 April, and it was switched off entirely on 15 May. Nobody lost work they needed and the tickets stayed manageable.

Adoption is not the problem. Almost everybody is in Teams and almost everybody is in it several times a day, because there is nowhere else to go.

The cost shows up somewhere else. When we surveyed the organisation a few weeks after shutdown, people were still asking for Slack back, and a number of them said communication in their area had got worse since the move. That is not nostalgia you can train away. It is the bill for a decision that was announced rather than explained, and for a rollout that was judged on whether the software worked.

The bit that transfers

Shipped and adopted are different states, and the gap between them is a product problem.

Everyone used the new tool immediately, which by any rollout metric is a success, and the collaboration it was supposed to improve got worse. If your measure of success is that people are logged in, you will never see that.

Which is what the whole thing exposed. Nobody owned the tools. They arrived because a team wanted one, never left because removing one requires somebody whose job it is to decide, and nobody measured whether any of them helped. There was a support queue, and a queue answers the question in front of it and has no opinion about whether the question should have existed.

So the work after the migration became building the thing that should have existed before it. The landscape is now run as a product domain rather than a support function. One person holds the whole picture including the budget and what gets retired. Every tool area has a named owner inside the company who owns its roadmap. Operations feeds what it sees upward instead of just closing tickets. Every tool goes through evaluation, a documented decision, an introduction, an embedding check, and eventually a shutdown, and writing the shutdown step down at the same time as the introduction step is what stops the mess reforming.

We have three months left to finish the setup, so this is a case study without an ending. Most areas have a named owner and the lifecycle has been used on a real decision. What is unproven is whether it survives us leaving.

The rule I would carry into the next rollout is that you do the discovery before the date, not after it. Every hard thing we hit was somebody's undocumented workflow, and all of it was findable in advance by asking. We just asked in June, and the date had been set in February.
